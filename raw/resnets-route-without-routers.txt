ABSTRACT
Residual networks execute every block for every input, yet their functional con-
tributions need not be input independent. We formulate a trained ResNet as a
set function over binary residual-branch masks and apply M¨obius inversion to de-
compose its output exactly into individual residual corrections and higher-order
interactions. For smooth residual stacks, we show that each fixedk-way inter-
action scales asO(λ k)under residual scaling. Exhaustive analysis of ImageNet-
pretrained ResNet-18 and ResNet-34 reveals that interaction mass peaks at orders
five and ten, respectively, rather than at low orders. Reducing the residual scale
shifts both spectra toward lower orders, but also changes model predictions. The
interaction coefficients are concentrated in magnitude but not hard sparse, and
prediction-preserving sparsity weakens with depth. Crucially, the dominant inter-
actions vary across inputs and predicted classes around a shared global core, while
their overall complexity changes little with sample difficulty. These results show
that dense ResNets implement an implicit form of soft routing: every block is ex-
ecuted, but different inputs rely on different residual interaction experts. Routing
can therefore emerge at the level of functional contribution without an explicit
router or sparse execution.
1 INTRODUCTION
Standard ResNets execute every residual block for every input and contain no explicit router, yet we
show that their functional contributions exhibit implicit input-dependent routing. Residual networks
enable the reliable optimization of deep models through identity shortcuts and residual updates (He
et al., 2016a). The influential path-ensemble view of Veit et al. (2016) interprets each block as
offering a shortcut and a residual branch, yielding exponentially many structural paths of differ-
ent lengths. This perspective helps explain network redundancy, robustness to block removal, and
training methods such as stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016).
The path-ensemble view is structural rather than functional. A masked-network response is the cu-
mulative output of a surviving nonlinear subnetwork, not the additive contribution of an isolated
path. Path counting therefore does not reveal how residual branches individually or jointly support
a prediction, or whether their dominant contributions vary across inputs. These questions connect
ResNets to conditional computation. Dynamic residual models explicitly gate blocks (Wang et al.,
2018; Wu et al., 2018), while sparse Mixture-of-Experts models activate selected experts for each in-
put (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022). Any routing in a standard ResNet
must instead arise at the level of functional contribution. We therefore ask:Do standard ResNets
exhibit implicit functional routing over residual interactions, despite having no explicit router?We
characterize such routing by contribution concentration and systematic input dependence of the
dominant interactions.
To answer this question, we treat a trained ResNet as an input-conditioned set function over bi-
nary residual-branch masks. Boolean M ¨obius inversion yields a unique, exact decomposition into
a shortcut-only baseline, individual residual corrections, and higher-order interactions (Rota, 1964;
Grabisch et al., 2000; Kang et al., 2024). We call high-contribution termsimplicit residual experts.

Our central finding is that dense execution does not imply a fixed functional pathway. In both
models, each input induces a different allocation of contribution over a shared dictionary of resid-
ual interactions. Dominant experts vary systematically across inputs, predicted-class signatures are
strongly class-structured, and full-logit signatures retain a shared global core. This establishes im-
plicit functional routing: ResNets reuse common interaction experts while changing which ones
dominate each prediction. The routing is soft rather than hard because smaller interactions remain
decision relevant, especially in ResNet-34, while expert complexity changes little with input dif-
ficulty. Standard ResNets therefore route functional responsibility across inputs despite executing
every residual block.
Our contributions are: (1) an input-conditioned residual-mask framework that provides a unique and
exact decomposition into individual corrections and higher-order interactions; (2) a coefficient-wise
O(λk)connection between residual scale and interaction strength; and (3) empirical evidence that
pretrained ResNets exhibit class-structured, readout-dependent implicit routing, organized around a
shared global core and input-specific expert emphasis. Together, these results show that routing can
emerge at the level of functional contribution without an explicit router or sparse execution.
2 BACKGROUND ANDTHEORETICALFRAMEWORK
To determine whether a trained ResNet exhibits input-dependent, routing-like specialization, we
distinguish structural paths from functional contributions. The path-ensemble view identifies which
computational paths exist, but not how residual branches jointly affect a particular prediction. For
each input, we therefore treat the network as a set function over binary residual-branch masks and
use M ¨obius inversion to decompose its output exactly into individual corrections and higher-order
interactions. We then test whether a small subset faithfully preserves the output and whether the
dominant interactions vary systematically across inputs. Evidence for both properties is required
to support a limited routing interpretation at the level of functional contribution rather than sparse
execution.

Input-dependent Implicit Routing.In sparse MoE models, a router selects a small set of experts
for each input. A standard ResNet has no explicit router. Nevertheless, the residual expert set
ArK(x)is input-dependent because the residual-mask responses depend on the input representation
at every block. Thus, two inputsx i andx j may have different effective expert sets:A rK(xi)̸=
ArK(xj). This motivates an implicit routing interpretation: the input does not explicitly choose
which residual experts to execute, but it induces which residual corrections and interactions are
functionally active. We can measure this input dependence by comparing top-Kexpert sets across
samples:J rK(xi, xj) =|A rK(xi)∩A rK(xj)|/|ArK(xi)∪A rK(xj)|. If samples from the same class or
with similar visual structure have higher overlap than unrelated samples, then residual interactions
exhibit a form of input-dependent specialization. This is the sense in which a trained ResNet may
behave like an implicit soft MoE: not by sparse execution, but by sparse, input-dependent functional
contribution.

3 FROMSTRUCTURALPATHS TOFUNCTIONALINTERACTIONS
This section empirically tests functional interactions behind the structural path view. We first show
that masked-subnetwork outputs are not additive path contributions, while their M¨obius coefficients
exactly reconstruct the logits. We then measure interaction energy across residual-branch orders
and find that pretrained ResNets concentrate mass at mid-to-high orders. Finally, we scale residual
branches at inference time to test whether smaller residual updates lower interaction order and how
much they change predictions.

5 RELATEDWORK
Our work intersects four lines of research. (1)Residual learning and path-ensemble views.ResNets
ease deep-network optimization via identity shortcuts (He et al., 2016a), and the unraveled view in-
terprets them as ensembles of exponentially many paths with ensemble-like redundancy (Veit et al.,
2016); related work studies stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016), the effective depth of the virtual
ensemble (Littwin & Wolf, 2016), and signal propagation (He et al., 2016b; De & Smith, 2020).
These studies concern structure and optimization, not which residual interactions produce a particu-
lar prediction. (2)Gating and dynamic routing.Highway networks (Srivastava et al., 2015), residual
gates (Savarese et al., 2016), SkipNet (Wang et al., 2018) and BlockDrop (Wu et al., 2018) equip
networks with explicit, learned gates or per-input block skipping. We instead analyze a standard
trained ResNet without adding a router, using gates only as an analytical probe over residual-branch
masks. (3)Mixture-of-Experts.Sparse MoE models scale capacity by routing each input to a few
experts (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Fedus et al., 2022), with subsequent work on
routing assignment (Lewis et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022) and cross-layer expert
paths (Gu et al., 2026; Rajbhandari et al., 2022). We approach MoE from the opposite direction:
rather than adding explicit experts, we ask whether a trained ResNet already induces implicit, input-
dependent expert structure. (4)Attribution and interaction decompositions.Component importance
has been estimated via Taylor criteria (Molchanov et al., 2019) and Shapley values (Lundberg &
Lee, 2017; Ancona et al., 2020), recently extended to higher-order interactions (Chen et al., 2023;
Muschalik et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2024). Our players are not input features, neurons, or filters but
residual branches; treating branch on/off as the game defines residual corrections and their higher-
order interactions as functional contribution units, bridging residual learning and implicit expert
behavior.

6 CONCLUSION
We use M¨obius inversion to decompose trained ResNets exactly into residual corrections and higher-
order interactions. In ResNet-18 and ResNet-34, interaction mass peaks at intermediate orders,
while residual scaling shifts it toward lower orders but also changes predictions. Crucially, dominant
interactions vary across inputs and classes around a shared global core. Dense execution therefore
does not imply fixed functional computation: every block executes, yet different inputs rely on
different interaction experts. Standard ResNets thus exhibit implicit soft routing at the level of
functional contribution, not explicit sparse execution. Extending this analysis to deeper networks
requires efficient estimators of high-order interactions.

B DETAILEDRELATEDWORK
Residual learning and path-ensemble views.Residual networks were introduced to ease the op-
timization of very deep neural networks by reformulating layers as residual functions with respect
to their inputs (He et al., 2016a). A residual block has the formx l+1 =x l +F l(xl), where the
identity shortcut provides a stable information and gradient pathway. A prominent interpretation
of this architecture is the unraveled view of Veit et al. (2016), which shows that a ResNet can be
represented as an exponential collection of computational paths of different lengths. Their lesion
studies suggest that these paths exhibit ensemble-like redundancy, and their gradient analysis shows
that relatively short paths dominate training. Stochastic depth (Huang et al., 2016) provides a related
training mechanism: during training, residual layers are randomly dropped, effectively exposing the
model to shorter subnetworks while retaining the full network at test time. Our work builds on this
line of thought but asks a different question. Beyond the original path-ensemble analysis of path
lengths, block-removal robustness, and gradient flow (Veit et al., 2016), subsequent work has exam-
ined how the effective depth of the virtual ensemble evolves during training (Littwin & Wolf, 2016)
and how identity shortcuts and residual-branch scaling affect signal propagation and trainability (He
et al., 2016b; De & Smith, 2020). These studies illuminate network structure and optimization, but
do not identify the residual interactions that contribute to a particular prediction. In contrast, we
study the functional contribution structure induced by residual branches. Rather than treating paths
as additive outputs, we introduce a residual-gate function view and ask whether residual corrections
and their higher-order interactions form input-dependent, sparse, expert-like computational units.
Thus, our focus is not merely that ResNets contain many paths, but whether their effective residual
computations behave like implicit soft experts.
Gating, dynamic routing, and residual branch selection.Highway networks (Srivastava et al.,
2015) introduced input-dependent gates to regulate information flow between transformed and
skipped representations. Residual gates (Savarese et al., 2016) further studied scalar gates on resid-
ual architectures, showing that explicit gating can facilitate optimization and increase layer indepen-
dence. Dynamic inference methods such as SkipNet (Wang et al., 2018) and BlockDrop (Wu et al.,
2018) learn to skip convolutional or residual blocks on a per-input basis, thereby converting static
residual networks into explicitly routed conditional-computation models. These methods introduce
or learn explicit gating mechanisms. Our goal is different: we analyze a standard trained ResNet
without adding a router. We use gates only as an analytical probe to define a function over residual
branch masks. This allows us to measure how the model output changes as residual branches are
activated or removed, and to characterize whether the induced contribution structure is sparse and
input-dependent.
Mixture-of-Experts and expert-path structure.Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models use gating
or routing functions to combine specialized expert modules (Shazeer et al., 2017). Sparse MoE
architectures such as GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) and Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022)
scale model capacity by activating only a small number of experts for each input. Subsequent
work studies load balancing and routing assignment, including BASE layers (Lewis et al., 2021),
Expert Choice routing (Zhou et al., 2022), and stable routing strategies (Dai et al., 2022). More
recent work has started to view MoE computation through the lens of cross-layer expert paths;
for example, Path-Constrained MoE constrains the combinatorial space of expert paths by sharing
router parameters across consecutive layers (Gu et al., 2026). Residual MoE architectures, such
as PR-MoE in DeepSpeed-MoE (Rajbhandari et al., 2022), also suggest that dense/shared paths
and sparse expert paths can be combined. Our work approaches MoE from the opposite direction.
Instead of adding explicit sparse experts to a network, we ask whether a trained residual network
already induces implicit expert-like structures. The analogy is not exact: a standard ResNet does not
perform sparse execution and has no explicit router. Nevertheless, if only a small subset of residual
corrections or interactions dominates the functional contribution for a given input, then the network
exhibits an implicit, soft, input-dependent expert structure.
Attribution, pruning, and interaction decompositions.A related line of work estimates the im-
portance of neural network components for pruning or interpretation. Taylor-based pruning methods
estimate the contribution of neurons or filters to the final loss (Molchanov et al., 2019), and Shapley-
value approaches provide principled component importance measures for structured pruning (An-
16
Preprint
cona et al., 2020). More broadly, Shapley values and related cooperative-game-theoretic tools have
become common for explaining model predictions (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). Recent work extends
these ideas to higher-order interactions: HarsanyiNet uses Harsanyi interactions to compute Shap-
ley values in a single forward propagation (Chen et al., 2023), Shapiq provides tools for estimating
Shapley interactions in machine learning (Muschalik et al., 2024), and the M ¨obius transform has
been proposed as a way to identify sparse, low-degree interactions in learned functions (Kang et al.,
2024). Our analysis is inspired by this interaction viewpoint, but the players in our setting are
not input features, neurons, or filters. Instead, we treat residual branches as players and study the
function induced by turning these branches on or off. This lets us define residual corrections and
higher-order residual interactions as functional contribution units. The resulting framework provides
a bridge between residual learning and implicit expert behavior.

E NAIVEADDITIVERECONSTRUCTION OFMASKEDRESPONSES
This appendix provides the complete experimental details for testing whether masked-subnetwork
responses can be interpreted as independent additive contributions. We compare three naive ag-
gregations of the same residual-mask evaluations with exact M ¨obius reconstruction on ImageNet-
pretrained ResNet-18 and ResNet-34. Across both architectures, summation, averaging, and
shortcut-baseline centering fail to recover the full-network logits, whereas M ¨obius inversion re-
constructs them to numerical precision. The experiment therefore distinguishes masked responses,
which are overlapping nonlinear interventions, from the additive interaction coefficients derived
from them.
E.1 MOTIVATION
The path-ensemble view characterizes a ResNet as a collection of structural paths, but it does not
assign independent additive outputs to those paths. In particular, the responseh x(1S)obtained by
activating a subsetSof residual branches is the output of an entire surviving nonlinear subnetwork.
It retains shared stem and shortcut computation, normalization, nonlinear activations, projection
shortcuts, and the final classifier, and a single mask may itself contain multiple structural paths.
Consequently, directly aggregating masked responses can repeatedly count shared and lower-order
effects.
We use naive aggregation as a diagnostic rather than as a claim about the classical path-ensemble lit-
erature. The question is whether masked responses nevertheless behave approximately like additive
contribution units:Can the logits of the full ResNet be reconstructed by directly summing, averag-
ing, or baseline-centering its masked-subnetwork responses?A negative answer motivates the use
of M¨obius coefficients, which remove repeated lower-order effects through inclusion–exclusion.

F INTERACTION-ORDERSPECTRUM
This section provides the full protocol and numerical results for the interaction-order analysis sum-
marized in Figure 2. We exhaustively evaluate all residual-branch masks of pretrained ResNet-18
and ResNet-34 models and examine how their M¨obius squared coefficient mass is distributed across
interaction orders. At the natural operating point,λ= 1, both models are dominated by mid-to-
high-order interactions: their spectra peak at orders5and10, respectively, above the corresponding
midpointsL/2 = 4andL/2 = 8.
F.1 MOTIVATION
The exact reconstruction experiment in Appendix E establishes that M ¨obius coefficients form an
additive decomposition of the residual-mask response. We next ask how the magnitude of these
coefficients is distributed across interaction orders. For a subsetS⊆[L], the order|S|counts the
number of residual branches involved in the corresponding interaction. It should not be confused
with structural path length: branches in a low-order interaction may be far apart in depth, while a
short structural path need not induce a low-order functional interaction.
Our diagnostic question is therefore whether a pretrained ResNet, at its natural operating point,
is dominated by individual and low-order residual corrections or by interactions involving larger
groups of branches. This experiment also establishes theλ= 1reference point for the residual
scaling intervention. Proposition 2.3 characterizes the attenuation of each fixedk-way coefficient as
λ→0; it does not require the unscaled network itself to be low-order dominated.

G RESIDUAL-SCALINGSWEEP

tion spectrum. For both ResNet-18 and ResNet-34, decreasing the residual scaleλmonotonically
reduces the effective interaction order and transfers squared coefficient mass toward orders1–3.

H TOP-KRESIDUAL-INTERACTIONRECONSTRUCTION
This section tests whether the exact residual-interaction dictionary is compressible at the level of in-
dividual terms. We rank the non-empty M¨obius coefficients separately for each input and reconstruct
the scalar predicted-class readout or the full logit vector from the largestKterms. The results reveal
a consistent distinction: squared coefficient mass is concentrated, but faithful output reconstruction
can require a substantially larger support. This distinction is modest for ResNet-18 and pronounced
for ResNet-34. The selected sets are post-hoc analytical supports and do not imply sparse execution.
H.1 MOTIVATION
The interaction-order spectrum in Appendix F describes how coefficient mass is distributed across
orders|S|, but it does not determine how many individual terms carry that mass. A mid- or high-
order spectrum may still be concentrated in relatively few subsets. We therefore ask:How many
input-specific interaction terms are required to capture the coefficient mass, approximate the logits,
and preserve the full model’s prediction?These are different criteria because signed scalar coeffi-
cients can cancel and vector-valued coefficients are not generally orthogonal.

I INPUT-DEPENDENTRESIDUALEXPERTSETS
This section tests whether the high-magnitude residual interactions identified for one input are also
selected for other inputs. Across both ResNet-18 and ResNet-34, vector-ranked interaction sets
contain a large shared global core, but same-class images consistently exhibit greater overlap than
different-class images. Decision-conditioned scalar sets show substantially stronger class alignment,
whereas confidence has little effect on set similarity. The resulting picture is therefore not one of
disjoint routes, but of class-structured, input-dependent variation around a broadly reused interaction
set.
I.1 MOTIVATION
Coefficient concentration alone does not establish input-dependent routing. A fixed set of large
interactions could explain every input and still yield a compressible representation. A routing-like
interpretation additionally requires the identities of the selected interactions to vary across inputs in
a structured manner. We therefore ask whether input-specific magnitude Top-Ksets differ across
images, whether same-class images share more terms than different-class images, and whether any
class gap survives random-set and label-shuffle controls. Because the underlying ResNets remain
densely executed, this is a test of input-dependent functional contribution rather than conditional
execution.

J SAMPLEDIFFICULTY ANDRESIDUALINTERACTIONCOMPLEXITY
This section examines whether difficult inputs induce a more complex residual interaction profile.
Across the studied ResNet-18 and ResNet-34 checkpoints, sample difficulty is only weakly asso-
ciated with interaction breadth, interaction order, or magnitude-based reconstruction budgets. The
hardest loss quartile exhibits a small increase in effective interaction count, most visibly for the
decision-conditioned scalar readout of ResNet-18, but the effective order and low-order coefficient
mass remain nearly unchanged. The same conclusion holds when misclassified and correctly clas-
sified images are compared. Thus, input dependence in the identities of selected interactions does
not translate into substantial difficulty-dependent scaling of the amount or order of functional inter-
action.
J.1 MOTIVATION
The preceding experiment shows that the identities of high-magnitude interactions vary across inputs
and contain class structure. That result does not establish that harder inputs use a broader or higher-
order set of interactions. A difficulty-adaptive interpretation would predict that hard images recruit
more interaction terms, shift coefficient mass toward higher orders, or require a larger magnitude-
ranked set to reconstruct the logits. We test these possibilities by relating per-image difficulty to
three complementary aspects of the residual-interaction decomposition: breadth, interaction order,
and magnitude-based compressibility. As throughout the paper, these quantities describe post-hoc
functional contributions rather than changes in the densely executed computation graph.

Conclusion.For the studied checkpoints, residual-interaction complexity is approximately stable

the overall breadth and order of the interaction profile remain nearly constant. This supports only
a limited routing-like interpretation at the level of functional contribution and does not support a
strong difficulty-adaptive interpretation or sparse execution.
