ABSTRACT
Understanding how cities evolve over long horizons is essential for capacity al-
location, operational scheduling, and emergency management. As horizons ex-
tend from hours to days, city-wide traffic states evolve continuously across tem-
poral phases, giving rise to complex spatiotemporal dynamics. Our analysis of
real-world traffic data identifies three interrelated challenges: delayed interactions
among nodes and their temporal accumulation, changing dependencies across
phases and days, and the joint representation of long-term periodicity and lo-
cal peak and trough variations. To address these challenges, we propose Delay-
Coupled Neural Dynamics (DeCoDy), a graph dynamics framework with time
delays for modeling extremely long-horizon urban evolution. DeCoDy addresses
them through Multi-Delay Interaction and Accumulation, Phase Evolution and
Adaptive Coupling, and Global and Local Residual Representation, while generat-
ing continuous future trajectories with a constrained full-horizon response solver.
We evaluate DeCoDy on four real-world urban traffic datasets with horizons from
8 hours to 7 days. DeCoDy achieves the lowest MAE in 14 of 16 dataset–horizon
settings and the lowest RMSE on all four datasets for 7-day forecasting, demon-
strating its effectiveness in modeling extremely long-horizon urban evolution.

INTRODUCTION
Spatiotemporal forecasting supports resource allocation, operational scheduling, and advance
decision-making by anticipating how urban systems evolve over time. As the prediction horizon
extendsZhang et al. (2024), the task increasingly requires modeling the continuous evolution of spa-
tially distributed states rather than only mapping historical observations to future values. Existing
approaches improve long-horizon forecasting through direct predictionShao et al. (2022a); Zeng
et al. (2023), periodic modeling, attention-based temporal dependency learningZhou et al. (2021);
Wu et al. (2021); Zhou et al. (2022); Liu et al. (2021); Nie et al. (2022); Zhang & Yan (2023); Lin
et al. (2024), or graph-based spatial interaction modelingShao et al. (2022b); Liu et al. (2023a);
Hu et al. (2025); Li et al. (2017); Song et al. (2020); Gao et al. (2023). However, from hours to
daysZhang et al. (2024); He et al. (2025), city-wide traffic states evolve continuously across loca-
tions, phases, and day boundaries, while recurring patterns coexist with local peak and trough vari-
ations. Capturing such multiscale and interconnected evolution remains challenging for extremely
long-horizon urban forecastingFang et al. (2025).
To better understand the challenges of modeling multi-day urban traffic evolution, we analyze real-
world traffic data, as shown in Figure 1. First, Figure 1(a) shows that neighboring nodes exhibit
different deviation onset times and durations within the same episode. Deviations at multiple nodes
overlap in time, while the target node also changes throughout this ongoing evolution. These ob-

viations remain active and overlap in subsequent periods. Second, Figure 1(b) shows continuous
traffic evolution across intraday periods and a day boundary: deviations persist across midnight,
while local lagged correlation patterns between nodes change over time. This indicates that traffic
states evolve continuously across phases and days, while inter-node dependencies vary with tempo-
ral conditions. Finally, Figure 1(c) shows that traffic flows on different dates share similar overall

fic evolution. (1) Delayed interactions and temporal accumulation. Neighboring nodes may change
asynchronously, while deviations persist into subsequent periods. Models therefore need to capture
interactions at different delays and their accumulated effects over time. (2) Evolving relationships

and day boundaries, where inter-node dependencies may change while earlier states continue to
influence subsequent evolution. Models must therefore adapt interactions to changing temporal
conditions while preserving state continuity. (3) Joint representation of global periodic patterns and
local peak and trough variations. Different dates often share similar overall profiles but differ sub-
stantially in local peaks and troughs. Models must preserve stable periodic structure while retaining
sufficient flexibility for date-specific local variations. Addressing these challenges within a unified
framework is essential for modeling urban evolution over extended horizons.
In this work, we propose DeCoDy (Delay-Coupled Neural Dynamics), a graph dynamics frame-
work with time delays for modeling multi-day urban traffic evolution. To capture delayed interac-
tions among nodes and their temporal accumulation, we introduce the Multi-Delay Interaction and
Accumulation module, which integrates state persistence with neighboring responses at multiple
delays to model future evolution. To accommodate evolving relationships across phases and days,
we introduce the Phase Evolution and Adaptive Coupling module, which combines historical con-
ditions, future calendar information, and background profiles to generate time-varying local driving
inputs and node couplings. To jointly represent global periodic patterns and local peak and trough
variations, we design the Global and Local Residual Representation module, which uses a periodic
background as a long-term reference and models dynamic deviations from it. Finally, a constrained
full-horizon response solver generates continuous multi-day trajectories while controlling numerical
solution error. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

RELATED WORK
2.1
DELAYED INTERACTIONS AND TEMPORAL ACCUMULATION
Existing spatiotemporal forecasting methods widely use graph structures to characterize dependen-
cies among spatial nodesLi et al. (2017). A common line of work learns spatial relationships from
data and combines them with temporal modeling to capture interactions among nodesJiang et al.
(2023b). More fine-grained approaches further construct localized spatiotemporal relations to dis-
tinguish dependencies that vary across temporal contextsHu et al. (2025). From a dynamical per-
spective, delayed neighboring states can also be incorporated into continuous state evolutionLong
et al. (2024), allowing interactions to occur at different temporal offsetsJiang et al. (2023a).
These studies suggest that spatial dependencies involve not only whether nodes are related, but also
when neighboring states influence one another. For multi-day forecasting, however, a single spatial
relationship or local delay alone is insufficient to describe how interactions at multiple delays and
a node’s own persistent response jointly accumulate in future trajectories. Integrating multi-delay
interactions among nodes with state persistence therefore remains an important problem in modeling
long-horizon urban traffic evolution.
2.2
EVOLVING INTERACTIONS AND STATE CONTINUITY ACROSS PHASES AND DAYS
Traffic relationships do not remain constant throughout the forecast horizon. Existing approaches
have explored adaptive spatial relationshipsJiang et al. (2023b); Wu et al. (2019); Bai et al. (2020),
time-dependent interaction structuresJiang et al. (2023a); Hu et al. (2025), and condition-specific
dependencies to account for changes in traffic dynamics over timeLiu et al. (2023a). Such studies
indicate that interactions among nodes may vary with temporal conditions rather than remaining
fixed throughout the forecasting process.
Multi-day evolution further spans multiple intraday phases and day boundaries, requiring models
to adapt inter-node relationships to changing temporal conditions while preserving the influence of
preceding states. A key question is therefore how to adjust local driving inputs and node interactions
over time without treating different phases as independent segments, thereby maintaining continuous
evolution across phases and days.
2.3
GLOBAL PERIODIC PATTERNS AND LOCAL VARIATIONS
Long-term traffic sequences typically contain recurring periodic structure alongside substantial date-
specific local deviationsWu et al. (2022). Existing studies have addressed this property through tem-
poral decompositionZhou et al. (2021; 2022); He et al. (2025), explicit periodic modelingWu et al.
(2022); Liu et al. (2021); Lin et al. (2024), and residual learning. These approaches generally sep-
arate relatively stable recurring patterns from time-varying components so that long-term regularity
and local changes can be modeled with different representations.
Such studies suggest that separating recurring structure from local variations benefits long-horizon
forecasting. However, decomposition alone does not specify how future local deviations evolve
continuously over time. Multi-day urban traffic evolution therefore requires a unified representation
that preserves a stable periodic reference while retaining sufficient flexibility for date-specific peak
and trough variations, allowing global recurring structure and local deviations to jointly shape future
trajectories.

4.1
DATASETS AND EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
Datasets. We evaluate DeCoDy on four real-world traffic datasets: PEMS04 and PEMS08 Guo et al.
(2019), SD Liu et al. (2023b), and Seattle Cui et al. (2019). All datasets are sampled at 5-minute
intervals and chronologically split into training, validation, and test sets in a 6:2:2 ratio.
Baselines and Experimental Settings. We compare DeCoDy with eight representative methods:
iTransformer Liu et al. (2024), Extralonger Zhang et al. (2024), TimeFilter Hu et al. (2025), Visi-
Fold Zhang et al. (2026), CycleNet Lin et al. (2024), STID Shao et al. (2022a), DST-Mamba He
et al. (2025), and FaST Fang et al. (2025). Forecast horizons are H ∈{96, 288, 864, 2016}, corre-
sponding to 8 hours, 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days. The 8-hour task uses L = 96, while the remaining
tasks use L = 288. A separate model is trained for each horizon. All methods use the same data
splits, node sets, and test windows while retaining their native input configurations. Models are
selected by validation MAE and evaluated using MAE and RMSE, with random seed 42. Further
details are provided in Appendix A.

Overall Forecasting Performance. Table 1 compares DeCoDy with eight baselines across four
datasets and four horizons. DeCoDy achieves the lowest MAE in 14 of 16 settings and the lowest
RMSE in 9 settings. For 7-day forecasting, it obtains the lowest RMSE on all four datasets and the
lowest MAE on three. On these three datasets, DeCoDy reduces MAE over the strongest baseline
by 8.90%, 6.10%, and 9.77% on PEMS04, PEMS08, and Seattle, respectively, demonstrating strong
performance over extremely long horizons.

CONCLUSION
This work studies extremely long-horizon urban traffic evolution and analyzes three key charac-
teristics of real-world traffic data, including delayed interactions among nodes and their temporal
accumulation, evolving relationships and state continuity across phases and days, and the coexis-
tence of global periodic patterns with local peak and trough variations. To address these challenges,
we propose DeCoDy, whose Multi-Delay Interaction and Accumulation, Phase Evolution and Adap-
tive Coupling, and Global and Local Residual Representation modules respectively model delayed
interactions over time, evolving inter-node dependencies across temporal phases, and global peri-
odic structure together with local variations. Constrained coupling parameterization and full-horizon
solution further support stable response evolution and controlled numerical solution error over ex-
tended horizons. Experiments on four real-world urban traffic datasets demonstrate strong perfor-
mance from 8 hours to 7 days, with DeCoDy achieving the lowest MAE in 14 of 16 dataset–horizon
settings and the lowest RMSE on all four datasets for 7-day forecasting.

A.2
HISTORICAL EXTERNAL CONDITIONS
DeCoDy uses historical weather and incident records to supplement traffic representations. Weather
inputs include temperature, humidity, wind speed, wind direction, precipitation, visibility, and pres-
sure, together with observation age and validity information. Incident inputs summarize the number
of matched incidents within a historical interval and the time since the most recent matched incident.
Node-level external conditions are constructed according to the data sources and spatial matching
protocols of each dataset.
All external inputs are restricted to the historical window before the forecast origin. Future inputs
contain only known calendar information, without actual weather or incidents from the forecast
interval. Missing external observations are handled using explicit masks, and their normalization
parameters are estimated only from valid observations in the training interval.
Incident data come from retrospectively compiled records, with historical conditions constructed
according to occurrence times. This setting is therefore a retrospective forecasting experiment based
on historical occurrence times, rather than a complete reconstruction of incident reports actually
received at the time of forecasting. In Seattle, incident matching represents event context within 500
meters of a node, without implying verified incidents on the same road and in the same direction.

A.3
BASELINE METHODS
We select eight comparison methods covering general multivariate forecasting, periodic modeling,
lightweight spatiotemporal representations, and long-horizon traffic forecasting.
1. iTransformer represents variable sequences as tokens and learns relationships among vari-
ables through attention.
2. Extralonger jointly models spatial and temporal factors for extra-long-term traffic fore-
casting.
3. TimeFilter constructs spatiotemporal relationships at the temporal-patch level and filters
fine-grained dependencies relevant to forecasting.
4. VisiFold organizes cross-time relationships through a temporal folding graph and uses node
visibility to handle larger traffic networks.
5. CycleNet explicitly learns recurring periodic patterns and predicts residuals after removing
them.
6. STID incorporates spatial and temporal identity embeddings into multilayer perceptrons to
construct a lightweight forecasting model.
7. DST-Mamba decomposes time series into seasonal and trend components and combines
Mamba with linear mappings to model long-range dependencies.
8. FaST uses a mixture-of-experts mechanism for long-horizon forecasting on large-scale
spatiotemporal graphs.
All baselines are trained and evaluated using our data splits, node sets, and forecasting tasks, while
retaining their native input configurations and corresponding training objectives. DeCoDy addition-
ally uses historical weather and incident conditions. The main comparison therefore reflects overall
forecasting performance under the current input configurations and cannot by itself attribute gains
separately to external information and model architecture.
A.4
IMPLEMENTATION AND TRAINING SETTINGS
For eight-hour forecasting, both the look-back window and forecast horizon are set to 96. For one-
day, three-day, and seven-day forecasting, the look-back window is 288 and the forecast horizons
are 288, 864, and 2016, respectively. An independent model is trained for each dataset–horizon
combination, with the random seed fixed at 42. The main DeCoDy settings are listed in Table 4.
A training round is defined by a fixed sample-presentation budget rather than a complete pass
through the training set. Training uses a predetermined stream of sampled windows, with progress
monitored on a fixed sparse set of validation windows. The first four rounds are excluded from
early-stopping patience accounting, and at least five rounds are completed. After training, candidate
checkpoints are compared using MAE on the complete validation set, and the selected checkpoint is
evaluated on the complete test set.
DeCoDy uses FP32 computation. Larger tasks use microbatches and gradient accumulation to con-
trol memory consumption while preserving the effective batch size. Background statistics remain
fixed during training, while the remaining forecasting parameters are jointly optimized using masked
MAE in original flow units.

D
SOLVER COMPLEXITY ANALYSIS
Let N denote the number of nodes, E the number of effective directed edges, K = |D| the number
of delays, H the forecast horizon, and J the number of full-horizon updates actually executed. The
following analysis concerns a single sample and treats conditional parameter generation separately
from response solution.
Graph message computation. Each update computes neighboring influences for every effective
edge, delay, and future time position, with computational complexity
O(EKH).
(22)
Local response computation. Node-local exponential responses are computed using zero-padded
FFTs. Across all nodes, the computational complexity per update is
O(NH log H).
(23)
Thus, after future driving inputs and coupling parameters have been generated, the response solver
executes J full-horizon updates at a computational cost of
O(J(EKH + NH log H)) .
(24)
This expression explicitly retains the iteration count J. The actual count depends jointly on the error
tolerance and coupling strength; finite-horizon solvability alone does not imply that it is a constant
independent of the forecast horizon.
Explicitly storing edge–delay coupling coefficients over the entire forecast horizon requires
O(EKH) memory, while node responses and FFT work arrays require O(NH) memory. Training
additionally stores conditional encodings and intermediate quantities for backpropagation through
the iterations.
Actual memory consumption depends on batch size, iteration count, and the
activation-checkpointing strategy.
Full-horizon updates jointly compute future positions through graph messages and convolutions,
but their theoretical operation count does not directly establish a wall-clock advantage. Appendix E
reports solver timings and numerical errors for the same response equation under fixed hardware,
precision, and batch size; these measurements do not constitute end-to-end speed comparisons be-
tween different forecasting models.

GRAPH CONSTRUCTION AND COUPLING INITIALIZATION
After removing self-loops, we form an undirected union by taking the maximum weight across the
two directions, then expand each connection into two effective directions. These directions do not
represent verified road travel directions. Coupling is initialized as

Correlations use valid pairs from the training interval only; entries with fewer than three valid pairs
or a near-zero correlation denominator are set to zero. Couplings are learnable after initialization,
and these correlations do not establish causality.
G.2
MISSING VALUES AND PERIODIC BACKGROUND
Missing traffic is filled with the training mean, becoming zero after standardization, and masks dis-
tinguish it from observed zero flow. Historical boundary residuals subtract the periodic background
from filled values and are not treated as observed data. The periodic background is estimated en-
tirely from training data. Missing weekly-slot statistics fall back successively to the corresponding
daily slot, the node mean, and the global mean.
G.3
TEMPORAL ENCODING AND RESPONSE SOLVING
Historical calendars use sine and cosine encodings of daily and weekly phases, giving four dimen-
sions. Future calendars use eight daily and three weekly Fourier harmonics, together with normal-
ized lead time k/H and the periodic-background shape to construct future queries. The FFT length
is the smallest power of two no smaller than 2H −1, yielding linear causal convolution.
Defaults are Jmin = 4, Jmax = 64, Jcheck = 4, and εraw = 0.05. Training backpropagates through
the updates actually executed; the periodic background and standardization statistics remain fixed.
