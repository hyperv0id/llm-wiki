###### Abstract

The attention operator in Transformers can be viewed as a two-layer *fast-weight MLP*, whose weights are dynamically instantiated from input tokens and whose width equals
sequence length NN.
As the context extends, the expressive capacity of such an NN-width MLP increases, but scaling
its
fast weights becomes
prohibitively expensive
for extremely long sequences.
Recently,
this
*fast-weight scaling* perspective has motivated
the
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) attention, which partitions the sequence into *fast-weight experts* and sparsely routes the tokens to them.
In this paper, we elevate this perspective to a unifying framework for a wide range of efficient attention methods by interpreting them as scaling fast weights through
routing and/or compression.
Then we propose a
compress-and-route strategy,
which compresses
the
NN-width MLP into a narrower one
using a
small set
of *landmark queries* and constructs
*deformable experts* by gathering top-kk activated key-value pairs for each landmark query.
We call this strategy a
*Mixture of Top-kk Activations* (MiTA), and refer to the resulting efficient mechanism as
*MiTA attention*.
Preliminary experiments on vision tasks demonstrate the promise of our MiTA attention and motivate further investigation on its optimization and broader applications in more challenging settings.
Code is available at [this URL](https://github.com/QishuaiWen/MiTA).

## 1 Introduction

Attention is the core operation of Transformers, which underpin today’s
success and wide application of deep learning.
Intuitively, attention learns to store the context as key-value associations
and retrieve this short-term memory via queries ([Bietti et al., 2023](#bib.bib4)).
However, such an all-to-all lookup paradigm incurs quadratic
computation and memory complexity in the sequence length,
thereby hindering its scaling to long sequences.
To this end, a plethora of efficient attention methods have been explored ([Zhang et al., 2025](#bib.bib55)).

In recent years,
several lines of work have converged on
a unifying viewpoint:
*the key-value
pairs
in full attention
can be viewed as
the fast weights of an MLP ([Schlag et al., 2021](#bib.bib36); [Han et al., 2025](#bib.bib22));
and the parameters of a two-layer MLP
can be viewed as
slow-weight key-value
pairs ([Geva et al., 2021](#bib.bib19)).*
Therefore, pursuing an efficient attention can be framed as
a *fast-weight scaling*
problem,
and draw inspiration from
slow-weight (i.e., parameter) scaling approaches,
such as
weight tying ([Wu et al., 2025a](#bib.bib49)),
model pruning ([Cheng et al., 2024](#bib.bib10)), and conditional computation ([Riquelme et al., 2021](#bib.bib34)).

![Refer to caption](2602.01219v1/fast_weight_scaling_v5.png)


Figure 1: Fast-weight scaling and its two scaling strategies.
As the context extends,
the width of the two-layer fast-weight MLP induced by full attention increases accordingly.
We categorize efficient fast-weight scaling
approaches into two strategies:
a) scaling by routing and b) scaling by compression,
and illustrate each of them with a representative method:
Mixture of Block Attention (MoBA) ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)) and
Test-time Training (TTT) ([Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)).
A pioneering method that combines both strategies is Native Sparse Attention (NSA) ([Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)).

Recent work along this direction has demonstrated that sparse attention inspired by Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) can
scale up the effective sequence length,
deliver wall-clock speedups,
and accommodate large-scale pretraining ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)).
In contrast to traditional MoE,
whose experts are structured, input-independent model parameters
(i.e., slow weights),
their experts are input-dependent fast weights ([Schmidhuber, 1992](#bib.bib37)),
more precisely, subsets
of
key-value pairs.
Routing each query to the fast-weight experts sparsely,
MoE attention
reduces the complexity from quadratic to linear
in sequence length NN.

Roughly speaking, the central challenge in scaling the fast weights via MoE
is to construct fast-weight experts from unstructured key-value pairs.
For example,
given the spatial and temporal locality priors in many modalities,
a straightforward, hardware-friendly approach is to partition the sequence into
contiguous, non-overlapping, fixed-size blocks,
and regard these blocks as fast-weight experts
(i.e., a mixture of blocks or chunks) ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54); [Wu et al., 2025b](#bib.bib50); [Cai et al., 2025](#bib.bib5)).
After that,
routing vectors are obtained by aggregating each block into a single vector,
e.g.,
via average pooling or parameterized modules.

Since splitting the NN-width fast-weight MLP into
a mixture of such blocks
is coarse and suboptimal,
subsequent work ([Jia et al., 2025](#bib.bib24)) has sought to improve upon the splitting scheme. Notably, top-kk attention ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31))
can be interpreted as
spawning NN kk-width
sub-MLPs (i.e., experts) from the NN-width MLP,
via
gathering top-kk key-value pairs attended (i.e., activated) by each query.
Therefore,
the fixed-shape expert in prior MoE attention methods are replaced by top-kk attention
with deformable experts. However, instead of the width of the fast-weight MLP in full attention,
it is the number of such deformable fast-weight experts that always equals NN in their top-kk attention.

On the other hand,
unlike the above methods that scale fast weights by accessing a subset of them,
linear attention ([Katharopoulos et al., 2020](#bib.bib27))
and Test-Time Training (TTT) ([Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib40))
compress the NN-width MLP into one (or several ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56)))
light-weight module(s),
analogous to model compression ([Ba & Caruana, 2014](#bib.bib1))
and knowledge distillation ([Hinton et al., 2015](#bib.bib23)).
Therefore,
from a fast-weight scaling perspective,
we divide
the scaling approaches of
existing efficient attention methods discussed in this paper
into two general categories: a) scaling by routing, and
b) scaling by compression
(see Fig. [1](#S1.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations") for an illustration).
Scaling by compression alone sacrifices a precise access to the original key-value pairs; whereas scaling by routing alone lacks a global summary of the full context.
Although these two approaches are not mutually exclusive, most
existing methods typically adopt only one of them.

In this paper, we elevate the fast-weight scaling perspective
into a five-dimensional taxonomy
that accommodates a broad spectrum of prior efficient-attention methods (see Tab. [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Contributions. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")).
While such a taxonomy is by no means complete,
it paves the way towards a unified framework.
Then, we propose to combine the two scaling strategies together and construct a tunable number of deformable fast-weight experts via Mixture of Top-kk Activations (MiTA),
which uses a small, tunable number
of *landmark queries* to look up key-value pairs. Specifically, MiTA
compresses the values
as *landmark values*,
yielding a compact key-value set (i.e., a narrower MLP)
as a shared expert,
in which landmark queries serve as compressed keys and landmark values
serve as compressed
values; and
reorganizes the original key-value pairs into deformable experts via gathering top-kk key-value pairs activated by each landmark query.
As shown in Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Contributions. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"),
MiTA attention concatenates
these compressed key-value pairs
with a routed, deformable subset of the original key-value pairs
for each query.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Fast-Weight Scaling

In contrast to slow weights (i.e., model parameters)
encoding persistent knowledge,
fast weights are input-conditioned and can be viewed as short-term memory ([Behrouz et al., 2024](#bib.bib2)),
playing an important role in
meta-learning ([Kirsch et al., 2022](#bib.bib28))
and in-context learning ([Chan et al., 2022](#bib.bib7)).
While most scaling efforts have focused on
expanding slow weights (e.g., increasing model width and depth) ([Kaplan et al., 2020](#bib.bib26); [Tan & Le, 2019](#bib.bib41)),
extending Transformers’ sequence length
turns out to implicitly scale fast weights.

For instance,
Test-Time Training (TTT) ([Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib40)) effectively
compresses
the
NN-width two-layer fast-weight MLP in full attention
into a smaller fast-weight module,
likewise for linear attention ([Katharopoulos et al., 2020](#bib.bib27))
into a linear layer ([Han et al., 2025](#bib.bib22))
and Linformer ([Wang et al., 2020](#bib.bib45)) into a narrower MLP.
Rather than scaling by compressing the full fast-weight MLP,
MoE attention factorizes it into experts
and access them sparsely via routing.

In our MiTA, the two strategies mentioned above—scaling by compressing and scaling by sparse routing—are combined.
It follows the Agent attention ([Han et al., 2024](#bib.bib21)) to compress
full key-value pairs
into a smaller set (i.e., a narrower MLP),
which offers a coarse yet global summary,
and then leverages the top-kk activations of the agent tokens to identify
underlying
experts,
which enables precise retrieval.

### 2.2 MoE-Inspired Sparse Attention

Previous sparse attention methods typically focus on
the design of fixed sparse patterns, such as
the local window and the vertical-slash pattern ([Jiang et al., 2024](#bib.bib25)),
or learnable ones, such as
hash buckets ([Kitaev et al., 2020](#bib.bib29)) and kk-means clusters ([Roy et al., 2021](#bib.bib35)).
To maintain a global connectivity,
shared memory
(i.e., tokens that attend to and are attended by all tokens)
is also introduced ([Beltagy et al., 2020](#bib.bib3)).
However, these patterns are either task-dependent ([Lai et al., 2025](#bib.bib30)) or
too burdensome to achieve wall-clock speedup ([Dao et al., 2022](#bib.bib15)).

Recently, sparse attention inspired by Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
has been applied to pre-training large language models,
yielding extended effective context with reduced overhead ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32); [Yuan et al., 2025](#bib.bib54)).
Its key advantage is the
routing mechanism,
which enables query-aware selection within the key-value cache.
Consequently,
despite coarse selection granularity
(e.g., evenly split, non-overlapping blocks),
the sparse pattern is highly flexible and can vary across queries.

In this paper,
we argue that, beyond routing,
the experts themselves can also be query-aware, i.e., deformable.
Specifically, for an arbitrarily long sequence,
we can build a fixed number of experts by gathering semantically related key-value pairs.
A similarly motivated line of research
is to replace
the fixed, non-overlapping rectangular patchification in ViTs ([Dosovitskiy et al., 2021](#bib.bib18))
with a
deformable,
content-adaptive tokenization scheme ([Chen et al., 2021](#bib.bib8)).

### 2.3 Deformable Attention

More broadly,
conditional computation, the idea underlying MoE,
has been explored through the deformable convolution ([Dai et al., 2017](#bib.bib13))
in convolutional neural networks.
And
deformable attention was introduced for object detection by
[Zhu et al. (2021)](#bib.bib59)
and later generalized to Vision Transformers
by
[Xia et al. (2022)](#bib.bib51).
Given a query, while being blind to keys,
deformable attention predicts offsets
relative to a small set of default positions of the keys,
thereby inducing a query-aware, deformable sparse attention pattern
(i.e., a fast-weight expert in our context).

Recent top-kk attention methods, e.g.,
spark attention ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)) and deepseek sparse attention ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)),
which have been applied to the training of Gemma 3n and DeepSeek-V3.2, respectively,
can also be viewed as improved deformable attention.
Instead of predicting the spatial positions solely from the query
as in prior deformable attention,
these methods locate the top-kk key-value pairs by
allowing each query to take a lightweight skim of the full keys, e.g.,
using partial features ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53)) or low precision (FP8) ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)).
Our MiTA retains the key advantage of the top-kk attention by
constructing deformable experts (i.e., sparse patterns)
that depend on both queries and keys.
However,
unlike the deformable attention methods discussed above,
we
replace the per-query offset predicting ([Zhu et al., 2021](#bib.bib59); [Xia et al., 2022](#bib.bib51))
or light-weight skim ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31))
with more efficient per-query routing.

## 3 Methods

This subsection will first present
the mathematical formulation of
the fast-weight MLP
in full attention,
and then reinterpret efficient attention
as a fast-weight scaling problem.
From this perspective,
a taxonomy of previous efficient attention methods
in terms of how they scale fast weights will be given.

Unbounded fast-weight scaling.
Because the hidden dimension of
the fast-weight MLP in Eq. ([3](#S3.E3 "Equation 3 ‣ 3.1 Efficient Attention as Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"))
always equals to the sequence length NN,
the per-query overhead grows linearly with NN rather than
fixed as in the slow-weight MLP in Eq. ([2](#S3.E2 "Equation 2 ‣ 3.1 Efficient Attention as Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")).
Thus, under the all-to-all lookup paradigm, processing NN queries incurs an overall 𝒪⁡(N2)\mathcal{O}(N^{2}) overhead.
This unbounded, rapid growth prevents scaling the fast weights to arbitrarily long sequences.
While this challenge is commonly described as the quadratic complexity of full attention
in the efficient-attention literature,
we refer to it as the *unbounded fast-weight scaling* issue.

Fast-weight scaling taxonomy.
Although we have discussed relevant efficient-attention methods in Sec. [2](#S2 "2 Related Work ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"),
they have not been systematically organized into a unified fast-weight scaling perspective.
We therefore propose a five-dimensional taxonomy:
a) *scaling strategy*, i.e.,
scaling the NN-width fast-weight MLP either by compressing it into a
light-weight
module or by routing each query to a subset of it;
b) *expert
count*,
i.e.,
how many fast-weight experts are constructed (in particular, scaling by compression typically yields a single shared expert);
c) *expert type*, i.e.,
what module each expert takes (e.g., an MLP or a linear layer);
d) *expert construction*, i.e., how experts are formed from the key-value pairs;
and
e) *routing topology*, i.e., the query-expert assignment pattern.
The mapping of existing methods to this taxonomy is summarized in Tab. [1](#S1.T1 "Table 1 ‣ Contributions. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").
We defer
the in-depth
discussion until Sec. [3.3](#S3.SS3 "3.3 Fit MiTA into the Fast-Weight Scaling Taxonomy ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").

### 3.2 Mixture of Top-kk Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention

Both scaling by compression and scaling by routing have inherent limitations.
Compressing the full fast-weight MLP inevitably discards information,
although the information loss can be mitigated by adopting milder compression schemes
(e.g., piecewise compression ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56)))
or by compressing into a more expressive module (e.g., via test-time training ([Sun et al., 2025](#bib.bib40))).
In contrast, scaling by routing is faithful and lossless, but it often lacks a global view of the context.

To combine the strengths of both strategies,
i.e., to retain a compact global summary while enabling precise, token-level retrieval,
we introduce a small set of *landmark queries* 𝑸~∈ℝd×m\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m} with m≪Nm\ll N.
These landmark queries probe the full key-value cache
and then, via cross-attention,
compress it into a global fast-weight module,
while simultaneously forming deformable fast-weight experts by gathering the top-kk activated key–value pairs for each landmark query.

This design is motivated by two observations:
a) register tokens can attend to distinct semantic regions (i.e., fast-weight experts) of an image ([Darcet et al., 2024](#bib.bib16));
and b) class embeddings ([Strudel et al., 2021](#bib.bib39)) (or object queries ([Carion et al., 2020](#bib.bib6)) and mask embeddings ([Cheng et al., 2021](#bib.bib9))) provide a compact global summary for dense prediction tasks ([Wen & Li, 2024](#bib.bib47)).
The landmark queries can be obtained
in various ways,
e.g., by assigning a set of learnable slow weights
or by downsampling the sequence using
a convolutional module.
In this work, we simply apply average pooling
over uniformly spaced, equal-sized windows,
as shown in Fig. [3](#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ 3.1 Efficient Attention as Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").

Specifically,
the ii-th landmark query 𝒒~i\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\_{i} defines the ii-th expert ℰi\mathcal{E}\_{i} as follows:

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝑲(i)\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{(i)} | =𝑲:,ℐi,𝑽(i)=𝑽:,ℐi∈ℝd×k,\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{K}\_{:,\mathcal{I}\_{i}},\ \boldsymbol{V}^{(i)}=\boldsymbol{V}\_{:,\mathcal{I}\_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}, |  | (6) |
|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ℐi\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\_{i} | =Topk⁡(𝑲⊤​𝒒~i)∈{1,…,N}k,\displaystyle=\operatorname{Top}\_{k}\left(\boldsymbol{K}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\_{i}\right)\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{k}, |  | (7) |

where 𝒒\boldsymbol{q} is a query routed to expert ℰi\mathcal{E}\_{i}, and
𝑲(i),𝑽(i)\boldsymbol{K}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{V}^{(i)} are the top-kk key-value pairs activated by 𝒒~i\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\_{i}.
This approach effectively reorganizes
the full key-value pairs into a Mixture of Top-kk Activations (MiTA)
(i.e., deformable fast-weight experts).
We directly use
𝒒~i\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\_{i} as the routing vector for expert ℰi\mathcal{E}\_{i}.
Then the routing logits from the NN queries 𝑸∈ℝd×N\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N} to the mm experts
is
𝑸⊤​𝑸~∈ℝN×m\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times m}.
We denote
the index of the rank-jj expert
that a query 𝒒\boldsymbol{q} is routed to
under these logits as ej​(𝒒)∈{1,…,m}e\_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

Moreover,
regarding the compressed global module, which can be viewed as
a shared expert ([Dai et al., 2024](#bib.bib12)),
we extract a set of *landmark values* 𝑽~∈ℝd×m\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m} via cross-attention
using the landmark queries.
The ii-th landmark value corresponding to the ii-th landmark query is given by:

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝒗~i=SDPA⁡(𝒒~i,𝑲,𝑽).\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{v}}\_{i}=\operatorname{SDPA}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{q}}\_{i},\boldsymbol{K},\boldsymbol{V}). |  | (8) |

Then, the shared expert ℰ~\tilde{\mathcal{E}} is defined as:

|  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ℰ~​(𝒒)=SDPA⁡(𝒒,𝑸~,𝑽~),\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{q})=\operatorname{SDPA}(\boldsymbol{q},\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}}), |  | (9) |

where landmark queries act as the keys in standard attention.
Note that the computations in Eqs. ([7](#S3.E7 "Equation 7 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")) and ([8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")) can be
largely shared,
where landmark queries are both querying the keys.
Likewise, Eq. ([9](#S3.E9 "Equation 9 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")) can reuse the routing logits.
Therefore, the computations of the two scaling strategies are tightly coupled in our MiTA.

Rather than a straightforward MoE implementation,
which isolates the computation within each expert
and aggregates their outputs
via a weighted sum,
we concatenate the experts as a single standard attention,
as shown in Fig. [2](#S1.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ Contributions. ‣ 1 Introduction ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").
Therefore, our
MiTA attention
can be written as:

|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | MiTA⁡(𝒒)\displaystyle\operatorname{MiTA}(\boldsymbol{q}) | =𝑽⋆​softmax⁡(𝑲⋆⊤​𝒒/d),\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}^{\star}\operatorname{softmax}({\boldsymbol{K}^{\star}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{q}/\sqrt{d}), |  | (10) |
|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝑲⋆\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}^{\star} | =[𝑸~,𝑲(e1​(𝒒)),…,𝑲(es​(𝒒))],\displaystyle=[\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}},\boldsymbol{K}^{(e\_{1}(\boldsymbol{q}))},\ldots,\boldsymbol{K}^{(e\_{s}(\boldsymbol{q}))}], |  | (11) |
|  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝑽⋆\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{\star} | =[𝑽~,𝑽(e1​(𝒒)),…,𝑽(es​(𝒒))],\displaystyle=[\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}},\boldsymbol{V}^{(e\_{1}(\boldsymbol{q}))},\ldots,\boldsymbol{V}^{(e\_{s}(\boldsymbol{q}))}], |  | (12) |

where 𝑲⋆,𝑽⋆∈ℝd×(m+k​s)\boldsymbol{K}^{\star},\boldsymbol{V}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(m+ks)} are the key-value pairs that a query 𝒒\boldsymbol{q} attends to in our MiTA,
and ss is the number of
routed
experts (not counting the shared expert) per query.
Note that, in practice, the attention can still be computed expert-wise and then combined,
thanks to the online softmax ([Milakov & Gimelshein, 2018](#bib.bib33)), as in the FlashAttention ([Dao, 2024](#bib.bib14)) implementation of [Lu et al. (2025)](#bib.bib32).

Our MiTA attention in Eq. ([10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"))
can be summarized as follows:
a) obtain landmark queries 𝑸~\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}} from queries 𝑸\boldsymbol{Q} via average pooling;
b) use landmark queries 𝑸~\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}} to query keys 𝑲\boldsymbol{K}, and hence
b.1) construct deformable fast-weight experts (i.e., 𝑲(i),𝑽(i)\boldsymbol{K}^{(i)},\boldsymbol{V}^{(i)}) via the top-kk activations
and b.2) extract landmark values 𝑽~\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}} via
cross-attention;
c) route queries 𝑸\boldsymbol{Q} sparsely to the experts, while keeping the shared expert (i.e, 𝑸~,𝑽~\tilde{\boldsymbol{Q}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{V}})
always active.

Complexity analysis.
The computational complexity of Eq. ([10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")) alone is 𝒪⁡(N⁡(m+k​s))\mathcal{O}(N(m+ks)),
whereas full attention incurs 𝒪⁡(N2)\mathcal{O}(N^{2}); in practice, N≫m+k​sN\gg m+ks.
Moreover,
the cross-attention
operations in Eqs. ([8](#S3.E8 "Equation 8 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")) and ([10](#S3.E10 "Equation 10 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"))
can be accelerated by the FlashAttention family ([Dao et al., 2022](#bib.bib15); [Dao, 2024](#bib.bib14); [Shah et al., 2024](#bib.bib38)).
The primary bottleneck comes from the gather operation in Eq. ([6](#S3.E6 "Equation 6 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations")):
it entails irregular (random) memory access
and is therefore likely to make the overall pipeline memory-bound.
Nonetheless,
Deepseek Sparse Attention
suggests that
this limitation can be mitigated via its optimized implementation ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31), Fig. 3).
Moreover,
compared with prior top-kk attention ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)),
which instantiate a private fast-weight expert per query
(NN experts in total)
without routing,
our MiTA uses a
*fixed* (or tunable)
number (i.e., mm) of fast-weight experts, which is more hardware-friendly,
at the cost of an NN-to-mm routing step, which is
explored in MoBA ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32)).
In summary,
our MiTA is composed of operations that have already been validated by engineering-centric prior work,
allowing us to focus on the fast-weight scaling perspective, its taxonomy, the combination of the two scaling strategies,
and the construction of a fixed number of deformable fast-weight experts.

### 3.3 Fit MiTA into the Fast-Weight Scaling Taxonomy

While MiTA is primarily motivated by combining the two complementary scaling strategies,
we find that it also
enriches
the remaining
dimensions of the
fast-weight scaling taxonomy proposed in Sec. [3.1](#S3.SS1 "3.1 Efficient Attention as Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").
In this subsection, we briefly discuss each of them.

Expert construction.
This
determines
the expert type and count, and hence the routing topology.
An important consideration
is what the construction conditions on:
the query only (e.g., DAT ([Xia et al., 2022](#bib.bib51))),
the key-value pairs only (e.g., MHLA ([Zhang et al., 2026](#bib.bib56))), or both.
When
using
key-value pairs,
the construction may be content-dependent and thus deformable (e.g., DSA ([Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31))) or
merely position-driven (e.g., MoBA ([Lu et al., 2025](#bib.bib32))).
While most efficient attention methods condition on one of the above aspects,
our MiTA
conditions on all of them
by extracting landmark queries from queries and
then probe the full key–value pool.
We illustrate a resulting benefit of this design in Fig. [4](#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ 3.2 Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations (MiTA) and MiTA Attention ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").

Expert type and count.
Expert types can be categorized in three classes:
a)
linear layers (e.g., linear attention),
b)
MLPs (e.g., sparse attention and PVT ([Wang et al., 2021](#bib.bib46))),
c)
arbitrary modules (e.g., test-time training).
The scaling-by-routing branch of MiTA is restricted to MLP experts, as in sparse attention.
However, the scaling-by-compression branch
can be implemented through test-time training, thereby generalizing
to other modules.
As for the expert count,
MiTA controls it via the
hyperparameter mm.
One benefit of this design has been discussed in
complexity analysis.
Another benefit is that it enables expert composition through the routing mechanism.

Routing topology.
In MiTA, there is a fixed number
of base sparse patterns (i.e., fast-weight experts),
which is a substantial improvement over efficient attention with only a single expert,
yet is still far fewer than prior top-kk attention ([You et al., 2025](#bib.bib53); [Liu et al., 2025](#bib.bib31)), which has NN experts.
Crucially, to compensate for this limitation,
MiTA
can resort to the routing mechanism to compose these mm base experts:
when routing each query to ss experts, the number of effective sparse patterns (i.e., the combinatorial number of experts)
is (ms)\binom{m}{s}.
In particular,
when s=1s=1,
routing may degenerate into clustering,
restricting information flow within each cluster.
We thus quantify the positional overlap between an expert’s gathered key–value pairs
and the queries routed to it.
As shown in Fig. [5](#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ 3.3 Fit MiTA into the Fast-Weight Scaling Taxonomy ‣ 3 Methods ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations"),
the overlap remains consistently modest across layers,
suggesting that MiTA with s=1s=1 performs routing rather than hard clustering.

Implementation details.

### 4.4 Generalization Across Model Designs

Unlike length extrapolation ([Zhao et al., 2024](#bib.bib57)),
we study MiTA’s generalization across the expert count mm and expert width kk.
Fig. [7](#S4.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ 4.3 Long Sequence Modeling and Wall-Clock Speed ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations") reports MiTA’s inference performance when interpolating within, and extrapolating beyond, the training (m,k)(m,k).
We find that MiTA is notably more robust to increasing mm and kk than to decreasing them.
A promising direction is to train MiTA with smaller mm and kk for efficiency, and then scale up mm or kk to obtain substantial gains.

Additionally, we explore the generalization of Transformers across attention mechanisms.
Given a checkpoint that learns slow weights under an attention mechanism,
we swap in a different attention mechanism at inference time and evaluate the resulting performance.
The results are reported in Fig. [8](#S4.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ 4.3 Long Sequence Modeling and Wall-Clock Speed ‣ 4 Experiments ‣ MiTA Attention: Efficient Fast-Weight Scaling via a Mixture of Top-𝑘 Activations").
We observe that checkpoints trained with MiTA transfer better to full attention.

## 5 Conclusion

We adopted a fast-weight scaling as a unifying perspective for efficient attention methods and introduced a five-dimensional taxonomy. Moreover, we proposed an efficient attention method, which is termed as the mixture of top-kk activations (MiTA),
to bridge the two
scaling strategies—–scaling by routing and scaling by compression–—and construct a tunable number of deformable experts.
We provided a detailed analysis of both the taxonomy and our MiTA, suggesting a promising path for developing efficient attention from the fast-weight scaling perspective.
